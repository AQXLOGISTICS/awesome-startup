# Why firing beats hiring for early stage teams

Notes from a 500 Startups session on early team composition.

## The core argument

Firing matters more than hiring, especially in early stage startups.

- Good teammates push you forward. Bad teammates you have to push.
- You cannot become an extraordinary leader without extraordinary teammates. Bad seeds do not grow into tall trees.
- If you are thinking about whether a teammate has problems, they do. If they were extraordinary, you would be thinking about their work instead of questioning whether they are good enough.
- Everyone needs to learn on a good team, not just the founders. If someone needs to be taught everything, they should not be there. The people you hire should save you time, not consume it.
- Many extraordinary startups become mediocre because the founders stay loyal to early teammates rather than to the company. People come and go across stages. Your responsibility as a founder is taking the company to its next success, not pleasing everyone.
- Do not compromise on early teammates. Work only with the best.

## Thoughts that mean the decision is already made

If you are asking yourself any of these about an early team member, the answer is to let them go:

- They have a good attitude but learn slowly. Should I give them more time?
- They are not good, but we cannot move forward without them. (Better to slow down and replace them.)
- Is it my management that made them inefficient? (You can always improve, but that is not why they are underperforming.)
- Am I putting too much pressure on them? (Extraordinary people know how to prioritize. If they do not, they are not the right fit.)
- Rehiring is expensive. (If you rehire you will probably survive. If you compromise, the startup is over.)

## How to manage in the meantime

- Always state why. Do not just cover what the work is and how to do it. Explain why, every day.
- Never fire suddenly. Say what you are dissatisfied with and be explicit that you will part ways if it is not fixed within a stated period.
- Distinguish good mistakes from bad ones. Good mistakes are first-time mistakes, and you should encourage them. Bad mistakes are the same mistake three or more times. You do not need teammates who make bad mistakes.

## The caveat

Managing humanely depends on having extraordinary teammates in the first place. Mediocre teammates drain the energy and confidence of everyone around them. B and C players rarely work well alongside A players. They are not the worst people available, but they are not the best, and on an early team there is no room for anyone who is not.
